US Affiliates Rule

BIS extends Entity List and MEU restrictions to companies 50% or more owned by listed parties, directly or indirectly, alone or together. Leading-edge tools for China already need a licence. The rule matters most for spares, consumables and service, and for the companies a listed chipmaker owns abroad.

Scope

The list stays the same length. What it covers does not.

Until now BIS had to name each affiliate it wanted to restrict. Under the rule, an unnamed company is caught by its owners. Holdings are added up across listed owners, and they pass down a chain through any company that is itself caught. The strictest owner's terms apply.

BIS's own example

Address listingsAffiliates of companies at an address on the list, unless those companies are named

When the ownership share cannot be found, the strictest terms of the listed owner apply. Not knowing is treated as the worst case.

Firm impact

A fab's owner, not its process node, decides whether its suppliers need a licence.

ItemContent
High exposureSuppliers and distributors of spares, consumables and service to fabs 50% or more owned by listed parties, mature-node fabs included; anyone dealing with a listed chipmaker's subsidiaries abroad
InformationThe fab's ownership chain, held by the customer and its local agents
CostOwnership tracing at every node; licences when ownership is unclear; service contracts on installed tools put on hold
AllocationExporters, reexporters and transferors carry strict liability. Ownership disclosure and change-notice clauses move the information, not the liability
No contract fixesA subsidiary in China that stops servicing a listed fab can breach China's Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law, State Council Decree 835 and a blocking order

Information trace

Ownership has to travel up the chain, from the fab to whoever ships. The node that ships most and knows least is the distributor.

{% include trace-table.html rule="affiliates" %}

BIS says the rule creates "an affirmative duty to determine the ownership of other parties to the transaction", and that the Consolidated Screening List "will no longer comprise an exhaustive listing". The search that BIS used to do by naming affiliates now falls on each exporter. It names freight forwarders as parties that may have obligations of their own.

The information also flows on to BIS. A licence application under the rule must name the listed owners, give their share and say how it was found, or explain why it could not be.

What the Entity List already shows

Before the rule, BIS was already listing intermediaries one name at a time.

~3,320
Entries on the Entity List on 15 October 2026. About 1,090 are under China, and about 290 of those have a Hong Kong address.
~160 / ~50
Entries whose names mark them as traders or as logistics firms. Distributors named "Technology Co." are not counted, so this is a floor.
18 of 19
Address-only entries that are offices in Hong Kong commercial buildings, several in the same building. BIS calls these addresses a diversion concern, citing "corporate secretarial services companies or logistics companies".
Two cases
A global distributorArrow's Hong Kong and China units were listed in October 2025 over US-origin parts found in drones used by Iran's proxies. The Hong Kong entry's addresses include units in two Kwai Chung cargo and logistics centres. Arrow China Electronics Trading was removed weeks later, reportedly after Arrow set out its affiliate structure and compliance measures. The Hong Kong entry was still on the list on 15 October 2026.
Subsidiaries in KoreaTwo of the five Korean entries are subsidiaries of Chinese chip firms: ACM Research Korea, of the cleaning-tool maker, and Empyrean Korea, of the chip design software maker. Under the rule, their own majority-owned companies would be caught without being named.

Counts are by keyword on entry names, from the eCFR text of Supplement No. 4 to Part 744. The list gives no reasons; those are in the Federal Register notice cited for each entry.
